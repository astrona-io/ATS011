# Section 090 Knowledge Check: Autogen Rules

Test your diagnostic reasoning before attempting the lab. Try to answer each question yourself before expanding the explanation.

---

**1.** You write a `ClusterPolicy` rule whose `match` block selects only `kind: Pod`, apply it with no special annotations, and then submit a non-compliant `Deployment`. What happens?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It is rejected. Kyverno's autogen controller automatically clones any rule that matches only `Pod` and retargets the clone at Pod-controller kinds — by default `DaemonSet`, `Deployment`, `Job`, `ReplicaSet`, `ReplicationController`, `StatefulSet`, and (via a separately-generated rule) `CronJob`. Your Deployment's Pod template is checked by the generated rule even though you never wrote `kind: Deployment` anywhere.
</details>

---

**2.** After applying a Pod-only `ClusterPolicy`, you run `kubectl get clusterpolicy <name> -o yaml` and look under `spec.rules`, expecting to find the autogenerated rule there. You don't see it. Where should you look instead, and why might older documentation have told you to look at `spec.rules`?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Look under `status.autogen.rules`. On current Kyverno releases, autogenerated rules are computed and reported separately from the policy you authored — `spec.rules` stays exactly as you wrote it. Older Kyverno versions spliced the generated rules directly into `spec.rules`, which is why some older material describes finding them there; this is exactly the kind of detail worth re-verifying against the version you're actually running rather than assuming from memory.
</details>

---

**3.** A Pod-only rule checks `metadata.labels.team`. What does the corresponding autogenerated rule check on a `Deployment`, and on a `CronJob` — and why are those two paths different from each other?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

On a `Deployment` (and `DaemonSet`, `Job`, `ReplicaSet`, `ReplicationController`, `StatefulSet`), the path becomes `spec.template.metadata.labels.team` — those kinds all hold their Pod template directly under `spec.template`. On a `CronJob`, the path becomes `spec.jobTemplate.spec.template.metadata.labels.team` instead, because a CronJob's Pod template sits one level deeper, inside a nested Job spec (`spec.jobTemplate`). That structural difference is exactly why CronJob gets its own separately-generated rule rather than being folded into the same one as the other six kinds.
</details>

---

**4.** What is the current mechanism for restricting which controller kinds receive an autogenerated rule, and what value completely disables autogen for a rule?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The `pod-policies.kyverno.io/autogen-controllers` annotation on the `ClusterPolicy`'s metadata, set to a comma-separated list of kinds (e.g. `Deployment` or `Deployment,Job`). Setting it to `none` disables autogen entirely for that policy — `status.autogen` then comes back empty (`{}`), and only the literal rule you wrote is evaluated.
</details>

---

**5.** You scope a Pod-only rule's autogen to `Deployment` only, explicitly excluding `Job`. You then submit a non-compliant `Job` whose Pod template has no `team` label. Is the `Job` object admitted? Does the Job ever actually run a Pod?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The `Job` object itself is admitted — no rule was generated for `Job`, so nothing intercepts the `Job` resource at creation. But the original Pod-matching rule still governs any bare `kind: Pod` submission, regardless of the annotation, and the actual Pod the Job controller tries to create is a bare Pod submission. That Pod gets rejected, repeatedly, and the Job sits there failing forever (`FailedCreate` events) without ever successfully running anything. Scoping autogen narrows which *controller objects* get their own generated rule; it never narrows what the original Pod rule matches.
</details>

---

**6.** A rule's `match.any` block lists both `Pod` and `Namespace` as `kinds` in the same entry. What does autogen do with this rule?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Nothing — autogen does not fire at all for this rule. `status.autogen` comes back empty. The moment a `match` (or `exclude`) entry names any kind other than `Pod`, Kyverno treats the rule's intent as too broad to safely rewrite and skips autogen entirely, rather than generating a partial or best-effort rule. If you need both a Pod-level check and a Namespace-level check, you must write them as two separate rules, each matching only its own single kind.
</details>

---

**7.** You write a Pod-only rule whose `pattern` already nests fields under `spec.template` (perhaps because you were thinking ahead to the Deployment you actually wanted to protect). Autogen still fires — what does the generated rule's rewritten path look like, and is it usable?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Autogen doesn't understand what your pattern means; it performs the same fixed, mechanical rewrite regardless of what's already there — wrapping whatever sits under `spec` one level deeper inside `spec.template`. If your pattern already had `spec.template.spec.containers`, the generated rule ends up checking `spec.template.spec.template.spec.containers` — a path that will never exist on a real Deployment. The generated rule is silently useless against every Pod-controller kind, even though it was generated without any error. The fix is to always write the Pod-only rule against the Pod's own native shape (`spec.containers`, not `spec.template.spec.containers`) and let autogen's rewrite produce the controller-shaped path for you.
</details>

---

**8.** True or false: because a `ClusterPolicy`'s `pod-policies.kyverno.io/autogen-controllers` annotation is metadata rather than a `spec` field, it has no effect on how the policy is actually enforced.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

False. Despite living under `metadata.annotations` rather than `spec`, this annotation directly controls the contents of `status.autogen.rules` — which controller kinds get a generated, enforced rule at all. It is read and acted on by Kyverno's policy controller exactly like a `spec` field would be; its placement under `metadata` is simply where Kyverno chose to expose this particular control, and it remains the current, documented mechanism rather than a legacy one superseded by a `spec` field.
</details>
